# Forms of poetry

## What is a poetic form?

- **Form** = the visible shape of a poem: its length, stanza pattern, rhyme scheme, metre, and purpose.
- Form is not decoration; it shapes meaning, mood, and reader expectation.
- BA exam MCQs often test: definition, line-count, rhyme scheme, metre, and a famous example of each form.

## Major forms at a glance

| Form | Core trait | Typical length | Key exam clue |
|------|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| Sonnet | 14 lines, lyrical argument | 14 lines | **iambic pentameter** |
| Elegy | Poem of loss/lament | Flexible | mournful, reflective tone |
| Ode | Celebratory, formal lyric | Variable | addressed to a person/thing |
| Epic | Long heroic narrative | Book-length | elevated style, invocation, supernatural machinery |
| Ballad | Folk narrative song | Short stanzas | dialogue, incremental repetition |
| Lyric | Personal emotion/song | Short | single speaker, musical |
| Dramatic monologue | One speaker reveals self | Flexible | silent listener, psychological |
| Allegory | Double meaning | Any length | characters/events = abstract ideas |

## Lyric, Sonnet, Elegy, Ode

### Lyric
- Originally meant to be **sung to a lyre**.
- Short, musical, expresses a single emotion or moment.
- Examples: most songs, Wordsworth's shorter poems, Keats's odes.
- BA MCQ trap: not every short poem is a lyric; it must feel like a song.

### Sonnet
- **14 lines**, usually in **iambic pentameter**.
- Two main types:
  - **Petrarchan/Italian**: octave (8 lines, ABBAABBA) + sestet (CDECDE or CDCDCD); often presents problem then resolution.
  - **Shakespearean/English**: three quatrains + a couplet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG); often ends with a twist or **volta**.
- The turn or **volta** usually shifts the argument.
- Famous sonnets: Shakespeare's Sonnet 116, Milton's *On His Blindness*, E. B. Browning's *How Do I Love Thee?*

### Elegy
- A **poem of mourning**; originally a specific metre in Greek/Roman poetry.
- Modern use: any reflective poem on death, loss, or transience.
- Key features: melancholy tone, meditation on mortality, consolation.
- Famous example: Thomas Gray's *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard*.

### Ode
- A **formal, celebratory lyric** addressed to a person, object, or abstract idea.
- Stately, complex stanzas; heightened language.
- Types: **Pindaric** (triadic stanzas), **Horatian** (regular, meditative), **irregular** (varied).
- Famous example: Keats's *Ode on a Grecian Urn*.

## Epic, Ballad, Dramatic Monologue

### Epic
- A **long narrative poem** recounting heroic deeds and national/cosmic significance.
- Conventions: invocation to the Muse, **in medias res** opening, catalogues, supernatural intervention, elevated diction.
- Examples: Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, Virgil's *Aeneid*, Milton's *Paradise Lost*.
- Exam clue: "heroic deeds + long + elevated style" = epic.

### Ballad
- A **short narrative folk song**, originally oral.
- Features: simple language, dialogue, repetition, incremental variation, tragic or supernatural theme.
- Stanza: usually **quatrains** with alternating lines of iambic tetrameter and trimeter (ABCB rhyme common).
- Examples: traditional ballads (*The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* is a literary ballad).

### Dramatic monologue
- A single speaker addresses a **silent listener**; the reader overhears.
- The speaker unintentionally reveals character, psychology, or moral flaw.
- Pioneered in English by **Robert Browning**.
- Famous example: *My Last Duchess*.
- Do not confuse with **soliloquy** (speaker alone on stage, e.g. Hamlet's speeches).

## Allegory and mixed forms

### Allegory
- A narrative in which **characters, events, or settings stand for abstract ideas**.
- Operates on two levels: literal story + symbolic meaning.
- Examples: Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress* (Christian allegory), Spenser's *The Faerie Queene*.
- Exam clue: if a poem/story is described as having a "double meaning," think allegory.

### Quick comparison: allegory vs. symbol

| Allegory | Symbol |
|----------|--------|
| One-to-one fixed meaning | Suggestive, open to many meanings |
| Entire narrative may be allegorical | Single image may be symbolic |
| Example: Christian in *Pilgrim's Progress* | Example: the albatross in Coleridge |

### Exam checklist
- Sonnet = **14 lines**; Shakespearean = **3 quatrains + couplet**.
- Elegy = **mourning/lament**.
- Ode = **celebration/address**.
- Epic = **long heroic narrative**.
- Ballad = **folk narrative song**.
- Lyric = **personal emotion**.
- Dramatic monologue = **one speaker + silent listener**.
- Allegory = **double meaning / characters as ideas**.
