Poetic devices

Structure, tone, and theme

Structure

  • The organisation of a poem: stanzas, line length, rhyme, metre, sequence.
  • A sonnet's structure (octave + sestet, or three quatrains + couplet) shapes its argument.

Tone

  • The poet's or speaker's attitude toward the subject.
  • Words like mournful, ironic, celebratory, satirical, reflective describe tone.
  • Tone is different from mood: tone is the speaker's stance; mood is the feeling the reader gets.

Theme

  • The central idea or message explored in the poem.
  • Common themes: love, death, nature, time, faith, identity, social justice.
  • A poem can have a surface subject and a deeper theme.

Rhythm, rhyme scheme, and metre

Rhythm

  • The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables that creates musical movement.
  • Regular rhythm = metre; irregular rhythm = free verse.

Rhyme scheme

  • The pattern of end rhymes in a stanza or poem.
  • Notated with letters: ABAB, AABB, ABBA.
  • Types:
  • End rhyme: rhymes at line endings.
  • Internal rhyme: rhymes within a line.
  • Slant rhyme: near-rhyme (e.g. soul / all).
  • Eye rhyme: looks like it rhymes but does not sound alike (e.g. love / prove).

Metre

  • The regular pattern of stressed/unstressed syllables.
  • Foot: basic unit. Common feet: iamb, trochee, anapest, dactyl, spondee.
  • Iambic pentameter: five iambs per line; dominant in English poetry.

Stressed and unstressed syllables

What is stress?

  • In English, some syllables are pronounced more strongly than others.
  • Poetry uses this natural stress to create metre.
  • Example: "to-DAY" = unstressed + stressed (iamb).

Line-length names

  • Monometer: 1 foot
  • Dimeter: 2 feet
  • Trimeter: 3 feet
  • Tetrameter: 4 feet
  • Pentameter: 5 feet
  • Hexameter: 6 feet

Figures of speech

Simile

  • Comparison using like or as.
  • Example: "My love is like a red, red rose."

Metaphor

  • Direct comparison without like/as.
  • Example: "Time is a thief."

Personification

  • Giving human qualities to non-human things.
  • Example: "The wind whispered through the trees."

Onomatopoeia

  • Words that imitate sounds.
  • Examples: buzz, hiss, clang, murmur.

Hyperbole

  • Exaggeration for effect.
  • Example: "I've told you a million times."

Alliteration

  • Repetition of initial consonant sounds.
  • Example: "whispering wind."

Assonance

  • Repetition of vowel sounds.
  • Example: "sleeP / crEEk / grEEn."

Consonance

  • Repetition of consonant sounds within or at the end of words.
  • Example: "piTTer paTTer."

Irony, inversion, negative capability, juxtaposition

Irony

  • Saying the opposite of what is meant, or a result opposite to expectation.
  • Types: verbal irony, situational irony, dramatic irony.
  • Often used by modern poets such as Philip Larkin.

Inversion

  • Reversing normal word order for emphasis or metre.
  • Common in poetry: "Up the hill did he climb" instead of "He climbed up the hill."
  • Also called anastrophe.

Negative capability

  • Coined by John Keats.
  • The ability to remain in uncertainty without rushing to reason or fact.
  • Important for understanding Keats's acceptance of mystery in poems like Ode on a Grecian Urn.

Juxtaposition

  • Placing contrasting images or ideas side by side.
  • Creates tension, contrast, or new meaning.
  • Example: youth beside age, beauty beside decay.

Exam checklist

  • Simile = like/as.
  • Metaphor = direct comparison.
  • Personification = human traits to non-human.
  • Onomatopoeia = sound-imitating word.
  • Irony = opposite meaning or outcome.
  • Negative capability = Keats + tolerating uncertainty.
  • Juxtaposition = contrast placed side by side.
