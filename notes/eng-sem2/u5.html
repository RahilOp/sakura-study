Gray, Wordsworth, Keats

Period overview: from Neoclassicism to Romanticism

  • This unit bridges the pre-Romantic sensibility of Gray and the full Romanticism of Wordsworth and Keats.
  • Romantic poetry values emotion, imagination, nature, the individual, and often reacts against industrialisation and rationalism.
  • Exam focus: identify each poet's central concern and the poetic form used.

Key Romantic ideas

  • Nature as spiritual and moral teacher.
  • The individual's inner experience.
  • Imagination over reason.
  • Common language and ordinary subjects.

Thomas Gray — Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard

Context

  • Gray is often called a transitional poet between Neoclassicism and Romanticism.
  • The poem is set in a rural churchyard at dusk.

Form

  • Elegy: meditative poem on death and transience.
  • Written in heroic quatrains (four-line stanzas in iambic pentameter, ABAB rhyme).

Main themes

  • Death makes all people equal; rich and poor share the same fate.
  • The "rude forefathers" buried there might have been great if given opportunity.
  • Social class and unrealised potential.

Famous ideas

  • The graves contain simple villagers who never had the chance to display genius.
  • The poem ends with the speaker imagining his own death and burial.

Exam points

  • Gray's Elegy is set in a country churchyard.
  • It reflects on death, equality, and lost potential.
  • It is a bridge between Neoclassical form and Romantic feeling.

William Wordsworth — The World Is Too Much with Us

Context

  • Wordsworth is a central Romantic poet and one of the Lake Poets.
  • This sonnet criticises materialism and loss of connection with nature.

Form

  • Petrarchan/Italian sonnet: octave + sestet.
  • Rhyme scheme: ABBAABBA CDECDE (or similar variation).

Main theme

  • Humans are too absorbed in "getting and spending"; we have lost our bond with nature.
  • The speaker would rather be a pagan and see gods in nature than live spiritually empty in modern society.

Key images

  • "The sea that bares her bosom to the moon."
  • Proteus and Triton from classical mythology — symbols of a more imaginative, nature-filled worldview.

Exam points

  • The poem is a Petrarchan sonnet.
  • It attacks materialism and celebrates nature.
  • Wordsworth is associated with Lyrical Ballads and the Lake District.

John Keats — Ode on a Grecian Urn

Context

  • Keats is one of the great Romantic poets, famous for his odes.
  • The poem addresses an ancient Greek urn painted with scenes of lovers, musicians, and a sacrifice.

Form

  • Ode: formal, celebratory lyric.
  • Five stanzas of ten lines each, with a complex rhyme scheme.

Main themes

  • Art captures beauty and freezes moments forever.
  • The figures on the urn are eternal but also unreal, unable to fulfil their desires.
  • The famous conclusion: "Beauty is truth, truth beauty" — art offers a kind of permanent truth.

Key paradoxes

  • The lovers will never kiss, yet their love will never fade.
  • The musician plays songs unheard, yet they are "ditties of no tone."
  • The urn remains silent but speaks across time.

Exam points

  • Ode on a Grecian Urn is by John Keats.
  • It explores the relationship between art, beauty, truth, and time.
  • Famous closing line: "Beauty is truth, truth beauty."

Romantic themes across the three poems

Exam checklist

  • Gray = transition to Romanticism; Elegy = churchyard, death, equality.
  • Wordsworth = Romantic; nature; materialism critique; Petrarchan sonnet.
  • Keats = Romantic ode; art/beauty/truth paradox; "Beauty is truth, truth beauty."
