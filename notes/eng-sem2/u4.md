# Shakespeare, Milton, Donne, Pope

## Period overview

- This unit covers three major English literary phases:
  - **Renaissance/Elizabethan** (Shakespeare)
  - **Metaphysical** (Donne)
  - **Neoclassical/Augustan** (Pope)
  - **Milton** spans Renaissance and Puritan periods.
- Exam MCQs often test the period, the form, and the central idea of each poem.

### Key literary labels
- **Renaissance poetry**: humanism, love, beauty, classical forms, sonnets.
- **Metaphysical poetry**: wit, conceits, philosophical argument, colloquial energy.
- **Neoclassical poetry**: reason, order, satire, heroic couplets, didactic purpose.

## William Shakespeare — Sonnet 116

### Context
- One of Shakespeare's best-known sonnets.
- Form: **Shakespearean/English sonnet** — three quatrains + a couplet, **iambic pentameter**.
- Rhyme scheme: **ABAB CDCD EFEF GG**.

### Main theme
- **True love is constant and unchanging**.
- It does not alter when it finds change; it is not "Time's fool."
- The poem argues against false love based on appearance or circumstance.

### Key images
- The **star** to every wandering bark (love as a fixed guide).
- **Time's scythe** — love is not affected by aging.
- The final couplet challenges anyone who disagrees: if this is wrong, the poet never wrote and no man ever loved.

### Exam points
- Sonnet 116 is a **definition/argument** about love.
- It is in **iambic pentameter**.
- Central claim: love is an "ever-fixed mark."

## John Milton — On His Blindness

### Context
- Written after Milton became blind.
- Form: **Petrarchan/Italian sonnet** — octave + sestet.
- Tone: personal, religious, ultimately resigned.

### Main theme
- How to serve God when one's talent seems useless because of disability.
- The poem moves from anxiety to acceptance.

### Key development
- The octave asks whether God expects active service from a blind man.
- The sestet answers: **"They also serve who only stand and wait."**
- Patience and quiet acceptance are also forms of service.

### Exam points
- "On His Blindness" is a **Petrarchan sonnet**.
- Famous closing line: "They also serve who only stand and wait."
- Theme: duty, faith, and acceptance of God's will.

## John Donne — Presence in Absence

### Context
- **Metaphysical poet**; poems combine passion with intellectual argument.
- Uses **conceits** — elaborate, surprising extended metaphors.
- Colloquial, dramatic tone; often argumentative.

### Main theme
- Love can make the absent lover feel present.
- Physical separation does not end spiritual/emotional union.

### Metaphysical features to remember
- Blending of physical and spiritual love.
- Unexpected comparisons (conceits).
- Direct address to the beloved or an imagined listener.
- Wit and paradox.

### Exam points
- Donne is a leading **Metaphysical poet**.
- His poems often use **conceits** and **paradox**.
- "Presence in Absence" explores love beyond physical distance.

## Alexander Pope — Essay on Man

### Context
- Written in **heroic couplets** (rhyming iambic pentameter pairs).
- A philosophical-didactic poem addressing human nature and the universe.
- Part of Pope's larger attempt to "vindicate the ways of God to man."

### Main themes
- The universe is a harmonious, ordered system; humans should accept their place.
- Pride leads humans to question God's design.
- "Whatever is, is right" — a famous, often debated conclusion.

### Style
- **Neoclassical**: reason, balance, antithesis, epigrammatic couplets.
- Satirical edge against human vanity.

### Exam points
- Pope wrote *Essay on Man* in **heroic couplets**.
- It is **Neoclassical** and didactic.
- Key idea: humans occupy a limited but meaningful place in a vast cosmic order.
