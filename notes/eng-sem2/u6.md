# Tennyson, E. B. Browning, Arnold, R. Browning

## Period overview: Victorian poetry

- The **Victorian age** roughly covers most of the 19th century.
- Poetry of this period often balances personal feeling with doubt, science, social change, and religious uncertainty.
- Common themes: loss, faith, love, duty, doubt, social critique.
- Exam focus: identify the poet, the form, and the typical Victorian tension in each poem.

---

## Alfred, Lord Tennyson — Break, Break, Break

### Context
- Tennyson was **Poet Laureate** for much of the Victorian era.
- This short lyric was written after the death of his close friend Arthur Hallam.

### Form
- **Short lyric**; irregular stanza length; musical repetition.
- The repeated "Break, break, break" mimics the crashing waves.

### Main theme
- Grief and the inability of nature to share human sorrow.
- The speaker sees happy life continuing around him while he mourns.

### Key contrast
- The eternal sea breaks on; human life and joy seem fragile.
- "But O for the touch of a vanish'd hand, / And the sound of a voice that is still!"

### Exam points
- Tennyson was **Poet Laureate**.
- *Break, Break, Break* is an **elegiac lyric** on personal loss.
- The sea symbolises both permanence and the speaker's unending grief.

## Elizabeth Barrett Browning — How Do I Love Thee?

### Context
- E. B. Browning was a major Victorian woman poet.
- This sonnet comes from her sequence *Sonnets from the Portuguese*.

### Form
- **Petrarchan/Italian sonnet**: octave + sestet.
- Rhyme scheme: ABBAABBA CDECDE.

### Main theme
- Counting the ways of loving someone.
- The speaker's love is spiritual, daily, lifelong, and extends beyond death.

### Key ideas
- Love as free and pure: "I love thee freely, as men strive for right."
- Love survives grief and will continue after death.

### Exam points
- The poem is from **Sonnets from the Portuguese**.
- It is a **Petrarchan sonnet** about the depth of romantic love.
- E. B. Browning was married to fellow poet **Robert Browning**.

## Matthew Arnold — Dover Beach

### Context
- Arnold was a poet and critic; this is his most famous poem.
- Set on the coast of England at Dover, looking across to France.

### Form
- **Dramatic lyric**: a single speaker addressing a companion.
- No strict stanza form; varies line length for conversational effect.

### Main theme
- The **loss of religious faith** in the modern world.
- The "Sea of Faith" was once full but is now retreating.
- The world seems beautiful but lacks certainty or spiritual meaning.

### Key images
- The calm sea, the moonlit cliffs, the grating roar of pebbles.
- The "melancholy, long, withdrawing roar" of faith leaving the world.

### Exam points
- *Dover Beach* reflects **Victorian doubt** and the decline of faith.
- The sea represents the ebbing of religious certainty.
- The speaker asks his companion to remain true to each other because the world offers no certainty.

## Robert Browning — My Last Duchess

### Context
- Robert Browning pioneered the **dramatic monologue**.
- The speaker is the Duke of Ferrara; he reveals himself while showing a portrait of his former wife.

### Form
- **Dramatic monologue**: one speaker, silent listener, unintended self-revelation.
- Written in **iambic pentameter couplets** (heroic couplets).

### Main theme
- Power, possession, jealousy, and the objectification of women.
- The Duke treats his wife as another art object to control.

### Key details
- The Duchess smiled too easily at everyone; the Duke saw this as disrespect.
- He implies he had her killed ("I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together").
- He is now negotiating to marry another woman.

### Exam points
- *My Last Duchess* is a **dramatic monologue**.
- The speaker is the **Duke of Ferrara**.
- It reveals pride, jealousy, and the abuse of power through the Duke's own words.
