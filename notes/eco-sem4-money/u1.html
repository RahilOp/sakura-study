Unit 1

Nature and functions of money

Definition of money

  • Money is anything generally accepted as a medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, and standard of deferred payment.
  • Modern view: money is defined by its functions rather than by a commodity form.

Traditional functions

Function Meaning Example
Medium of exchange Eliminates barter's double coincidence of wants Buying groceries with cash
Measure of value Common unit to quote prices Prices in rupees
Store of value Wealth can be held for future use Savings account
Standard of deferred payment Debts are settled in money units Loans repaid in rupees

Modern/additional functions

  • Transfer of value: moving wealth from place to place.
  • Basis of credit: loans and banking depend on money.
  • Liquidity: money is the most liquid asset.

Evolution of money

Stages in evolution

  1. Commodity money: goods like salt, cattle, grain used as money.
  2. Metallic money: gold and silver coins.
  3. Paper money: currency notes backed by metal or government authority.
  4. Credit money: cheques, drafts, bank deposits.
  5. Electronic/digital money: cards, UPI, cryptocurrencies, central bank digital currency.

Classification of money

  • Full-bodied money: intrinsic value equals face value (e.g. gold coins).
  • Token money: face value exceeds intrinsic value (e.g. coins, notes).
  • Legal tender: money that must be accepted for payment of debts.
  • Fiat money: issued by government order, not backed by commodity.
  • Near money: highly liquid assets that are not cash (e.g. treasury bills, fixed deposits).
  • Virtual money: digital or electronic forms of value.

Gresham's Law

  • "Bad money drives out good money."
  • When two forms of money circulate at a fixed exchange rate, people hoard the better form and spend the worse one.
  • Applies mainly under bimetallism or fixed exchange arrangements.

Demand for money

Classical approach

  • Money is only a medium of exchange.
  • Demand for money is proportional to the volume of transactions.
  • Quantity theory of money explains the value of money.

Quantity theory of money

Fisher's cash transaction approach

  • MV = PT
  • M = money supply
  • V = velocity of circulation
  • P = price level
  • T = volume of transactions
  • Assumes V and T are constant in the short run; price level is directly proportional to money supply.

Cambridge cash balance approach

  • Focuses on demand for money as a stock people choose to hold.
  • Md = kPY
  • k = fraction of income held as money
  • P = price level
  • Y = real income
  • Emphasises the store-of-value function.

Keynesian demand for money

  • Demand for money arises from three motives:
  • Transactions motive: daily spending.
  • Precautionary motive: emergencies.
  • Speculative motive: future bond-price/interest-rate expectations.
  • Total demand is called liquidity preference.

Friedman's modern approach

  • Money demand depends on permanent income, returns on bonds and equities, and expected inflation.
  • Money is one of many assets in a portfolio.

Value of money and role in different economies

Value of money

  • The purchasing power of money; inverse of the price level.
  • Inflation reduces the value of money; deflation increases it.

Role in capitalist economies

  • Money facilitates market exchange and capital formation.
  • Credit and financial markets channel savings into investment.

Role in developing economies

  • Money helps monetise traditional barter economies.
  • Banks and financial institutions mobilise savings for development.
  • Stable money is essential for planning and investment.

Exam checklist

  • Money's four main functions: medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, standard of deferred payment.
  • Evolution: commodity → metallic → paper → credit → electronic.
  • Gresham's Law: bad money drives out good.
  • Fisher: MV = PT.
  • Cambridge: Md = kPY.
  • Keynes: transactions, precautionary, speculative motives.
  • Liquidity preference = demand for money.
