Central banking

Definition

  • A central bank is the apex monetary authority of a country.
  • It regulates the banking system, issues currency, and manages monetary policy.
  • It acts as banker to the government and lender of last resort to commercial banks.

Main functions

  1. Currency issue: monopoly over issuing notes.
  2. Banker to the government: maintains government accounts and manages public debt.
  3. Bankers' bank: holds reserves of commercial banks and lends to them.
  4. Lender of last resort: provides emergency funds to banks in crisis.
  5. Controller of credit: uses monetary tools to regulate money supply and credit.
  6. Custodian of foreign exchange reserves: manages balance-of-payments and exchange rate.

Reserve Bank of India (RBI)

Establishment and structure

  • Established in 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act.
  • Nationalised in 1949; fully owned by the Government of India.
  • Head office in Mumbai.

Objectives

  • Maintain price stability.
  • Ensure adequate credit flow for productive purposes.
  • Promote financial stability and economic growth.
  • Regulate and supervise the financial system.

Functions

  • Formulates and implements monetary policy.
  • Issues currency notes.
  • Regulates banks and non-banking financial companies.
  • Manages foreign exchange under FEMA.
  • Acts as banker to central and state governments.

Quantitative methods of credit control

These affect the overall volume of credit in the economy.

Tool How it works
Bank rate / repo rate Rate at which RBI lends to banks; higher rate reduces credit
Open market operations (OMO) RBI buys/sells government securities to adjust money supply
Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) Percentage of deposits banks must keep with RBI; higher CRR reduces lendable funds
Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) Percentage of deposits banks must hold in liquid assets; affects credit creation
  • A rise in any of these generally tightens credit and reduces money supply.
  • A fall expands credit and increases money supply.

Qualitative/selective methods of credit control

These affect the direction and purpose of credit.

Tool How it works
Margin requirements RBI fixes the percentage of loan value that must be financed by the borrower; higher margin reduces speculative credit
Regulation of consumer credit Controls instalment terms for consumer durables
Selective credit controls Restrictions on credit for specific sectors or commodities
Moral suasion RBI persuades banks to follow desired lending policies
Direct action Penalties or restrictions on banks that do not comply

Indian money market

Meaning

  • The market for short-term funds (up to one year).
  • Instruments: call money, treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit.

Components

  • Organised sector: banks, financial institutions, RBI.
  • Unorganised sector: money lenders, indigenous bankers, chit funds.

Limitations of Indian money market

  • Seasonal fluctuations in demand for funds.
  • Dominance of unorganised sector in some areas.
  • Limited integration between different segments.
  • Concentration in major cities.
  • Dependence on RBI for liquidity.

Money market reforms

  • Development of call money market, repo market, and commercial paper.
  • Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) by RBI.
  • Electronic trading platforms.

Exam checklist

  • Central bank: apex monetary authority; issues currency, controls credit, banker to government.
  • RBI established 1935, nationalised 1949.
  • Quantitative controls: bank/repo rate, OMO, CRR, SLR.
  • Qualitative controls: margin requirements, moral suasion, selective credit controls.
  • Money market = short-term funds market; limitations include seasonality and unorganised sector.
