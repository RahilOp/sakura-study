Fiction I

Indian English fiction: an overview

  • Indian English novels often engage with social realities: caste, poverty, colonialism, gender, and rural life.
  • The two novels in this unit are classics that combine realism with political critique.
  • Exam focus: identify the author, central character, social issue, and narrative technique.

Kamala Markandaya — Nectar in a Sieve

Context

  • Kamala Markandaya was an Indian English novelist.
  • Nectar in a Sieve (1954) is her best-known novel.
  • It depicts rural Indian life before and after industrialisation.

Plot

  • The novel follows Rukmani, a village woman, and her husband Nathan.
  • They face drought, flood, poverty, and the loss of their land.
  • Their sons leave for the city; one daughter, Ira, turns to prostitution to feed the family.

Themes

  • Rural poverty and resilience: Rukmani endures repeated disasters.
  • Impact of industrialisation: a tannery disrupts village life and land ownership.
  • Family and gender: women's sacrifices and limited choices.
  • Colonial and capitalist transformation: traditional agrarian society under pressure.

Narrative style

  • First-person narration by Rukmani.
  • Simple, lyrical prose.
  • The title refers to a line from Samuel Taylor Coleridge's poem: "Work without hope draws nectar in a sieve."

Exam points

  • Nectar in a Sieve is by Kamala Markandaya.
  • Protagonist: Rukmani.
  • Key themes: rural poverty, industrialisation, family, resilience.

Mulk Raj Anand — Untouchable

Context

  • Mulk Raj Anand was a major Indian English writer associated with the Progressive Writers' Movement.
  • Untouchable (1935) is a powerful critique of the caste system.

Plot

  • The novel follows one day in the life of Bakha, a young sweeper belonging to an "untouchable" caste.
  • He faces humiliation, violence, and social exclusion while doing sanitation work.
  • The novel presents different solutions to caste oppression: Gandhism, conversion, and modern technology.

Themes

  • Caste oppression and dehumanisation.
  • Dignity and resistance: Bakha's desire for respect and self-worth.
  • Social reform: Gandhi's message, missionary Christianity, and the flush toilet as a technological fix.

Narrative style

  • Realist, detailed, and sympathetic.
  • Focus on a single day to intensify the experience.
  • Interior monologue shows Bakha's thoughts and humiliation.

Exam points

  • Untouchable is by Mulk Raj Anand.
  • Protagonist: Bakha.
  • It critiques the caste system and explores reformist solutions.
