Fiction II

New Literatures in English: fiction

  • This unit covers novels from South Africa, the Caribbean/Indian diaspora, and Northeast India.
  • These texts explore colonialism, identity, displacement, postcolonial nationhood, and gender.
  • Exam focus: identify the author, setting, protagonist, and major theme.

Nadine Gordimer — A Guest of Honour

Context

  • Nadine Gordimer was a South African novelist and Nobel laureate.
  • Her novels explore apartheid, race, and political transition.
  • A Guest of Honour (1970) is set in a fictional African country moving toward independence.

Plot and theme

  • The protagonist, James Bray, is a British colonial official invited back after independence.
  • He becomes caught between the new government, old colonial powers, and revolutionary forces.
  • The novel explores the complexities of decolonisation and political idealism.

Key concerns

  • Postcolonial politics: the gap between liberation ideals and practical governance.
  • Race and identity: Bray's position as a white outsider in a black nation.
  • Loyalty and betrayal: personal friendships tested by political change.

Exam points

  • Gordimer is South African.
  • A Guest of Honour examines post-independence politics and colonial aftermath.
  • Themes: decolonisation, race, political idealism.

V. S. Naipaul — A House for Mr Biswas

Context

  • V. S. Naipaul was a Trinidadian-British writer of Indian descent; Nobel laureate.
  • A House for Mr Biswas (1961) is considered his masterpiece.
  • It is partly based on the life of Naipaul's father.

Plot and theme

  • Mohun Biswas, born into a poor Indian family in Trinidad, struggles for independence and identity.
  • His deepest desire is to own a house of his own, symbolising dignity and autonomy.
  • The novel traces his failures, comic mishaps, and eventual partial success.

Key concerns

  • Postcolonial identity: Indians in the Caribbean, caught between cultures.
  • Individual vs family/society: Biswas rebels against the domineering Tulsi family.
  • The house as symbol: security, selfhood, and belonging.
  • Colonial mimicry and ambition: education and small ambitions in a colonial society.

Style

  • Tragicomic; detailed realism.
  • Rich portrayal of Indo-Trinidadian community life.

Exam points

  • Naipaul is Trinidadian-British of Indian descent.
  • Protagonist: Mr Biswas.
  • Central symbol: the house.
  • Themes: postcolonial identity, individualism, family oppression.

Indira Goswami — The Moth-Eaten Howdah of the Tusker

Context

  • Indira Goswami was an Assamese writer and activist.
  • The Moth-Eaten Howdah of the Tusker is set in Assam and explores the lives of widows in a satra (Vaishnavite monastery).

Plot and theme

  • The novel depicts the social and sexual exploitation of Brahmin widows.
  • It critiques religious orthodoxy, patriarchy, and caste practices in Assamese society.
  • The "moth-eaten howdah" symbolises decaying tradition and the decline of feudal/religious institutions.

Key concerns

  • Gender oppression: widows are denied autonomy and dignity.
  • Religious hypocrisy: the satra fails to protect the vulnerable.
  • Social reform: Goswami's work contributed to real debates about widows' rights in Assam.

Exam points

  • Indira Goswami is an Assamese writer.
  • The novel exposes the plight of Brahmin widows.
  • Setting: Assam; institution: Vaishnavite satra.
