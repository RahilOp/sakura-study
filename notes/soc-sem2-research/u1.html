Measurement and scales

Meaning and scope of social research

  • Social research is the systematic study of social phenomena using scientific methods.
  • It helps us understand social patterns, test theories, and inform policy.
  • Research involves observation, measurement, analysis, and interpretation.

Steps in research

  1. Selecting a topic.
  2. Reviewing existing literature.
  3. Formulating a hypothesis or research question.
  4. Choosing a research design.
  5. Collecting data.
  6. Analysing data.
  7. Reporting findings.

Conceptualisation and hypothesis

Conceptualisation

  • Defining abstract concepts clearly so they can be studied.
  • Example: "poverty" can be defined as income below a certain threshold, lack of assets, or multidimensional deprivation.

Hypothesis

  • A testable statement predicting a relationship between variables.
  • Example: "Higher education is associated with lower fertility."
  • Must be clear, specific, and capable of being supported or rejected by evidence.

Variables

  • Independent variable: presumed cause.
  • Dependent variable: presumed effect.
  • Example: education (independent) → income (dependent).

Scales of measurement

Nominal scale

  • Categories without order.
  • Examples: gender, religion, nationality, blood group.
  • Only counts and percentages are meaningful.

Ordinal scale

  • Categories with rank order but unequal intervals.
  • Examples: class rankings, socio-economic status, Likert scales.
  • Median and percentiles are useful.

Interval scale

  • Equal intervals between values but no true zero.
  • Example: temperature in Celsius.
  • Addition and subtraction are meaningful; ratios are not.

Ratio scale

  • Equal intervals and a true zero.
  • Examples: income, weight, height, age.
  • All arithmetic operations are meaningful.

Data: classification and presentation

Classification

  • Grouping data into categories for analysis.
  • Helps simplify large amounts of information.

Tabulation

  • Arranging data in rows and columns.
  • Frequency tables show how often each value occurs.

Graphical presentation

  • Bar graph: for categorical data.
  • Histogram: for continuous data.
  • Pie chart: for proportions.
  • Line graph: for trends over time.

Measures of central tendency

Mean

  • Arithmetic average.
  • Sensitive to extreme values.

Median

  • Middle value when data are ordered.
  • Useful when there are outliers.

Mode

  • Most frequently occurring value.
  • Useful for categorical data.

Choosing a measure

  • Use mean for symmetrical interval/ratio data.
  • Use median for skewed data.
  • Use mode for nominal data or most common value.

Dispersion

Meaning

  • Dispersion shows how spread out the data are.

Common measures

  • Range: highest value minus lowest value.
  • Mean deviation: average distance from the mean.
  • Standard deviation: most widely used measure of spread.
  • Variance: square of standard deviation.

Interpreting standard deviation

  • Larger SD = more spread out.
  • Smaller SD = more clustered around the mean.

Exam checklist

  • Research = systematic study of social phenomena.
  • Hypothesis = testable prediction; variables = independent and dependent.
  • Four scales: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio.
  • Central tendency: mean, median, mode.
  • Dispersion: range, mean deviation, standard deviation, variance.
