Intelligence testing

What is intelligence?

  • Intelligence refers to the ability to learn, reason, solve problems, and adapt to new situations.
  • It includes verbal, numerical, spatial, memory, and reasoning abilities.
  • Definitions of intelligence vary across theories and cultures.

Intelligence quotient (IQ)

Early formula

  • IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100
  • Mental age (MA): age level at which a person performs on a test.
  • Chronological age (CA): actual age in years.

Example

  • A 10-year-old child who performs like a 12-year-old has an IQ of (12/10) × 100 = 120.

Limitations

  • Mental age does not increase indefinitely.
  • The formula becomes less useful for adults.

Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales

Background

  • Developed by Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon in France; revised in the USA by Lewis Terman at Stanford University.
  • One of the earliest and most influential intelligence tests.

Features

  • Individual test administered by a trained examiner.
  • Includes a variety of tasks measuring memory, reasoning, vocabulary, and problem-solving.
  • Originally used the mental-age formula; later editions use deviation IQ.

Wechsler Intelligence Scales

Tests

  • WAIS: Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale.
  • WISC: Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children.
  • WPPSI: Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence.

Features

  • Uses deviation IQ: compares an individual's score to the average of their age group.
  • Includes verbal and performance subtests.
  • Provides an overall IQ and separate index scores.

Deviation IQ

  • Mean = 100, standard deviation = 15.
  • A score of 100 is average; 115 is one SD above average; 85 is one SD below.

IQ distribution

Normal curve

  • IQ scores follow an approximately normal distribution.
  • Most people score near the average (100).
  • Fewer people score very high or very low.

Culture-fair tests

Need

  • Traditional IQ tests may be biased toward certain cultural and linguistic backgrounds.
  • Culture-fair tests aim to reduce this bias.

Examples

  • Raven's Progressive Matrices: uses abstract patterns; requires little language.
  • Cattell's Culture Fair Intelligence Test.

Limitations

  • Complete cultural neutrality is difficult.
  • Test-taking skills, motivation, and familiarity with testing can still affect scores.

Issues in intelligence testing

Criticisms

  • IQ tests may measure only narrow academic abilities.
  • They can reflect education and opportunity rather than innate potential.
  • Heredity vs environment debate continues.
  • Misuse of IQ scores can lead to discrimination.

Ethical use

  • Tests should be administered and interpreted by trained professionals.
  • Scores should be one part of a broader assessment.

Exam checklist

  • IQ originally = (MA/CA) × 100.
  • Stanford-Binet and Wechsler are major intelligence tests.
  • Wechsler uses deviation IQ; mean = 100, SD = 15.
  • WAIS for adults, WISC for children, WPPSI for preschoolers.
  • Culture-fair tests: Raven's Progressive Matrices.
  • IQ distribution is normal; most scores cluster around 100.
