# Validity and reliability

## Validity

### Meaning
- **Validity** refers to whether a test or measure actually assesses what it claims to assess.
- A valid measure is accurate and meaningful.

### Types of validity

#### Face validity
- The measure appears to assess the intended concept at surface level.
- Example: a maths test looks like it measures maths ability.

#### Content validity
- The measure covers all aspects of the concept being studied.
- Example: a job-satisfaction questionnaire includes pay, work environment, colleagues, and growth.

#### Criterion-related validity
- The measure predicts or correlates with an external criterion.
  - **Concurrent validity**: correlates with a current measure.
  - **Predictive validity**: predicts future performance.

#### Construct validity
- The measure relates to other variables in theoretically expected ways.
- Example: anxiety scores should correlate with stress but not with unrelated traits.

---

## Reliability

### Meaning
- **Reliability** refers to the consistency of a measure.
- A reliable test gives stable results across time, items, and raters.

### Types of reliability

#### Test-retest reliability
- Consistency of scores when the same test is administered twice.
- High correlation between two administrations = high reliability.

#### Split-half reliability
- Divide the test into two halves and compare scores.
- High correlation between halves indicates internal consistency.

#### Internal consistency
- Measures whether all items assess the same construct.
- **Cronbach's alpha** is a common statistic; values above 0.70 are generally acceptable.

#### Inter-rater reliability
- Consistency between two or more raters scoring the same behaviour.
- Important for observational and projective tests.

---

## Validity vs reliability

- A test can be **reliable without being valid** (consistent but measuring the wrong thing).
- A valid test is usually reliable, because random error would reduce validity.
- Ideal measurement is both valid and reliable.

### Example
- A weighing scale that always shows 2 kg extra is reliable (consistent) but not valid (not accurate).

---

## Standardisation

### Meaning
- **Standardisation** means administering and scoring a test in the same way for everyone.
- Includes uniform instructions, conditions, time limits, and scoring keys.
- Ensures comparability of results.

### Norms
- Standardised tests provide norms based on a representative sample.
- Norms allow comparison of an individual's score to the broader population.

---

## Exam checklist

- Validity = accuracy; reliability = consistency.
- Types of validity: face, content, criterion-related, construct.
- Types of reliability: test-retest, split-half, internal consistency, inter-rater.
- A measure can be reliable without being valid, but valid measures are usually reliable.
- Standardisation ensures uniform administration and scoring.
