Personality tests

What is personality?

  • Personality refers to the relatively stable patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving that characterise an individual.
  • Personality tests attempt to measure these patterns.

Two major approaches

  • Trait approach: personality is made up of stable traits (e.g. extraversion, neuroticism).
  • Dynamic/psychodynamic approach: personality is shaped by unconscious conflicts and motives.

Self-report inventories

Meaning

  • Questionnaires in which respondents rate statements about themselves.
  • Objective, standardised, and easy to score.

Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)

  • One of the most widely used personality tests.
  • Originally designed to identify psychological disorders.
  • Contains validity scales to detect faking or inconsistent responding.

NEO-PI (NEO Personality Inventory)

  • Based on the Big Five personality traits:
  • Openness
  • Conscientiousness
  • Extraversion
  • Agreeableness
  • Neuroticism

Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ)

  • Measures three major dimensions:
  • Extraversion-Introversion
  • Neuroticism-Emotional stability
  • Psychoticism-Socialisation

Strengths of self-report inventories

  • Standardised and quantifiable.
  • Efficient for large samples.
  • Easy to compare across individuals.

Limitations

  • Respondents may fake or give socially desirable answers.
  • Requires reading ability and self-awareness.
  • May not capture unconscious aspects of personality.

Projective techniques

Meaning

  • Present ambiguous stimuli and ask respondents to interpret them.
  • Assumption: people project their unconscious feelings, motives, and conflicts onto the stimuli.

Rorschach Inkblot Test

  • Developed by Hermann Rorschach.
  • Respondents describe what they see in symmetrical inkblots.
  • Responses are scored for location, content, and determinants.

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

  • Developed by Henry Murray and Christiana Morgan.
  • Respondents create stories about ambiguous pictures.
  • Used to assess motives, needs, and conflicts.

Sentence Completion Test

  • Respondents complete incomplete sentences.
  • Example: "My greatest fear is ____."
  • Reveals attitudes, conflicts, and concerns.

Strengths of projective tests

  • Can access unconscious material.
  • Less susceptible to faking.
  • Useful in clinical settings.

Limitations

  • Scoring and interpretation are subjective.
  • Reliability and validity are often lower than objective tests.
  • Requires extensive training to administer and interpret.

Objective vs projective tests

Feature Self-report inventories Projective techniques
Questions Structured Ambiguous stimuli
Scoring Objective Subjective
What is measured Conscious traits and symptoms Unconscious motives and conflicts
Examples MMPI, NEO-PI, EPQ Rorschach, TAT, Sentence Completion
Reliability Usually high Variable

Exam checklist

  • Self-report inventories: MMPI, NEO-PI (Big Five), EPQ.
  • Big Five traits: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism.
  • Projective tests: Rorschach inkblots, TAT, Sentence Completion.
  • Projective tests rely on ambiguous stimuli and subjective interpretation.
  • Objective tests are standardised and easy to score but can be faked.
