# Inflation and the Phillips curve

## Aggregate demand and aggregate supply

### Aggregate demand (AD)
- Total demand for goods and services at different price levels.
- **AD = C + I + G + (X − M)**.
- Downward-sloping: higher price level reduces real spending power.

### Aggregate supply (AS)
- Total output firms are willing to produce at different price levels.
- **Short-run AS**: upward-sloping because wages and prices are sticky.
- **Long-run AS**: vertical at potential/full-employment output because prices and wages fully adjust.

### Changes in equilibrium
- Increase in AD (with unchanged AS): price level and output both rise in the short run.
- Decrease in AS (supply shock): price level rises while output falls.
- In the long run, output returns to potential; only price level changes.

---

## Determinants of inflation

### Inflation
- A **sustained rise** in the general price level over time.
- Measured by price indices such as CPI, WPI, and GDP deflator.

### Demand-pull inflation
- Caused by **aggregate demand exceeding aggregate supply**.
- "Too much money chasing too few goods."
- Common in booming economies or after large fiscal deficits.

### Cost-push inflation
- Caused by rising costs of production: wages, raw materials, energy prices.
- Reduces AS and raises prices while lowering output.
- Can lead to **stagflation**: high inflation + high unemployment + low growth.

### Other sources
- Imported inflation (rising import prices).
- Exchange-rate depreciation.
- Excessive money supply growth.
- Inflationary expectations becoming self-fulfilling.

## Inflation-unemployment trade-off

### The Phillips curve
- Named after **A. W. Phillips**.
- Originally showed an **inverse relationship** between wage inflation and unemployment in the UK.
- Later interpreted as a trade-off between **inflation and unemployment**.

### Short-run Phillips curve
- Downward-sloping: lower unemployment can be achieved at the cost of higher inflation.
- Based on sticky wages and unexpected inflation.

### Long-run Phillips curve
- Monetarists, led by **Milton Friedman**, argued the trade-off disappears in the long run.
- Expectations adjust; unemployment returns to its **natural rate**.
- Long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.

### Adaptive vs rational expectations
- **Adaptive expectations**: people base expectations on past inflation.
- **Rational expectations**: people use all available information; policies may be anticipated and neutralised.

---

## Stagflation

### Definition
- A combination of **stagnation (low growth)**, **high unemployment**, and **high inflation**.
- Contradicts the simple Phillips curve trade-off.

### Causes
- Adverse supply shocks (oil-price rise, crop failure).
- Cost-push pressures.
- Structural rigidities in the economy.

### Policy dilemma
- Expansionary policy fights unemployment but worsens inflation.
- Contractionary policy fights inflation but worsens unemployment.

---

## Inflation control policies

### Monetary policy
- Central bank reduces money supply or raises interest rates.
- Tight credit reduces aggregate demand.

### Fiscal policy
- Government reduces spending or raises taxes.
- Reduces budget deficit and demand-pull pressures.

### Supply-side policy
- Improves productivity, infrastructure, and competition.
- Reduces cost-push inflation.

### Incomes policy
- Wage and price controls; often used in emergencies.
- Can be effective short-term but distort markets.

---

## Exam checklist

- AD = C + I + G + (X − M); AS = total output supplied.
- Short-run AS slopes up; long-run AS is vertical at potential output.
- Demand-pull inflation = excess AD; cost-push inflation = rising costs.
- Phillips curve: short-run inverse inflation-unemployment trade-off.
- Long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
- Stagflation = high inflation + high unemployment + low growth.
